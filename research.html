Study Design

The DCIMIT project comprises three complementary studies designed to provide comprehensive insights into cervical cancer disparities among immigrant populations in Italy

Project Desing and Objective

Three sub-studies will be conducted

PROJECT RATIONAL

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide. In Italy, an estimated 2,382 new cases were reported in 2024. Nearly 99% of cervical cancer cases are caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV), a very common virus transmitted primarily through sexual contact. In most cases, the infection clears up on its own, but if it persists over time, it can develop into cancer. Fortunately, this is a highly preventable cancer.

In Italy, the HPV vaccine is free for girls and boys between the ages of 12 and 26. Women between the ages of 25 and 64 are encouraged to participate in screening programs through Pap tests or HPV tests. However, access to these programs is uneven: in the 2021-2022 period, only 77.7% of eligible women participated in screening, with an even lower percentage among immigrant women (72%).

Immigrants make up more than 8.6% of Italy's resident population. Many immigrant women face language barriers, financial difficulties, and limited knowledge of the healthcare system—factors that hinder their participation in screening and follow-up care, increasing the risk of late diagnoses and, consequently, higher mortality rates.

STUDY DESIGN

This project investigates inequalities in cervical cancer prevention among immigrant women in Italy through three complementary substudies: a mixed-methods study, a registry-based study, and a community-based intervention study. The goal is to reduce the incidence of cervical cancer among immigrant women, promote greater participation in screening programs, and provide useful guidance for developing more equitable and inclusive public health strategies.

The research project seeks to answer three key questions:

  1. What are the main barriers hindering early diagnosis of cervical cancer in immigrant women?
  2. Are there differences in the incidence, mortality, and survival rates of cervical cancer between immigrant women and Italian women?
  3. Can community-based interventions help immigrant women participate more in cervical cancer screening programs?
