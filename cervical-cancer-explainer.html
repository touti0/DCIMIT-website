01 · Anatomy

What is the cervix?

The cervix is the narrow passage connecting the uterus (womb) to the vagina. It's roughly the size of a walnut, and it opens during menstruation and childbirth.

Nearly all cervical cancers begin in the thin layer of cells covering the cervix — which is exactly what a routine screening test checks.

02 · Definition

What is cervical cancer?

It happens when cells on the cervix change and begin growing and dividing out of control. About 99% of cases are linked to long-term infection with high-risk types of HPV (human papillomavirus).

It isn't sudden — these cell changes typically build up slowly, over many years.

03 · Scale

How common is it?

Cervical cancer is the 5th most commonly diagnosed cancer in women globally. Around 604,000 new cases and 280,000 deaths occur each year.

The burden falls hardest on countries with less access to HPV vaccination and screening — a gap in access, not inevitability.

04 · Prevention

Why early detection saves lives

Cervical cancer usually develops slowly, over 10 to 20 years, passing through precancerous stages long before it becomes cancer. Regular screening — a Pap test or HPV test — can catch those changes while they're still easy to treat.

Caught early, cervical cancer is one of the most preventable and treatable cancers there is.

05 · An important distinction

Infection is not the same as cancer

HPV is extremely common — most people who are sexually active will get it at some point. In roughly 9 out of 10 cases, the immune system clears the infection naturally within one to two years.

Only when a high-risk type persists for years can it progress toward precancer — and, if it's never caught, cancer. Having HPV is not a cancer diagnosis.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

Get screened. Pap and HPV tests catch changes early, when they're most treatable.

Get vaccinated. The HPV vaccine prevents most of the infections that lead to cervical cancer.

Know the difference. HPV infection is common and usually clears on its own — it isn't a cancer diagnosis.

Talk to a doctor or clinic about screening and vaccination — it's one of the most effective cancer-prevention tools we have.
