Understanding HPV

A six-part guide

Understanding HPV

You've probably heard the name. Here's what it actually is.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

What is HPV?

HPV is a family of more than 200 related viruses. Most types are harmless; a smaller group of high-risk types can lead to cancer if left unchecked.

It's a completely different virus from HIV or herpes, despite the similar-sounding names.

02

How is it transmitted?

Through skin-to-skin contact during vaginal, anal, or oral sex — not through blood. It can be passed even with no visible symptoms.

03

Is HPV an STI?

Yes — the most common STI there is. Nearly all sexually active people get it at some point, even with only one partner, ever.

04

Can it disappear naturally?

Yes. About 9 in 10 infections clear on their own within one to two years, with no symptoms or lasting effects.

05

Does it always cause cancer?

No. Only a subset of high-risk types can, and only if the infection persists for years without clearing. Most HPV never leads to cancer.

06

HPV in men and women

It affects everyone. In the U.S. it's linked to about 43,000 cancers a year — roughly 25,000 in women (mostly cervical) and 19,000 in men (mostly throat).

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

It's common. Most sexually active people get HPV at some point.

It usually clears. About 9 in 10 infections resolve on their own.

It's not always risky. Only persistent high-risk types can lead to cancer.

Talk to a doctor about HPV vaccination and, for women, routine screening.

Sources: CDC, NCI (2024–2026). General information, not medical advice.

The HPV Vaccine

A four-part guide

The HPV Vaccine

One of the biggest sources of confusion online. Here's what's true.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

Who should receive it?

Recommended for boys and girls from age 11–12 (as early as 9). Catch-up vaccination is recommended through age 26.

Two doses if started before 15; three doses if started later.

02

Is it safe?

Yes — one of the most closely studied vaccines in use, with over 15 years of safety data across hundreds of millions of doses.

It does not cause infertility. That's a persistent myth with no supporting evidence.

03

Can adults still get vaccinated?

Yes, up to age 45, through a shared decision with a doctor — the benefit is smaller since many adults have already had some HPV exposure.

04

Does it replace screening?

No. It doesn't cover every cancer-causing type and can't treat an existing infection.

Vaccinated women still need routine cervical screening.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

It's for everyone. From age 11–12, with catch-up through 26.

It's safety-tested. Extensively studied, mild side effects.

It doesn't replace screening. The two work together, not instead of each other.

Talk to a doctor about vaccination — for yourself or your children, at any eligible age.

Sources: CDC, NCI (2024–2026). General information, not medical advice.

Why Screening Matters

A four-part guide

Why Screening Matters

Probably the single most important idea in preventing cervical cancer.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

What screening means

Checking for early warning signs — like precancerous cells or HPV — in people who feel completely healthy, before anything is wrong.

02

Why no symptoms still means screen

Early cervical changes usually cause no symptoms at all. Feeling fine isn't the same as being in the clear.

03

Prevention vs. diagnosis

Diagnosis confirms a disease that's already there. Prevention catches changes early enough that disease never develops.

04

Why it prevents, not just detects

Screening finds and removes precancerous cells before they ever become cancer — genuinely different from finding cancer that already exists.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

It's for the healthy, too. No symptoms needed — that's the whole point.

It stops cancer before it starts. Treating precancer prevents cancer entirely.

Waiting for symptoms is the risk. By then, it's often no longer prevention.

If you're due for screening, book it — you don't need to feel worried to justify going.

Sources: WHO, CDC, USPSTF (2024–2026). General information, not medical advice.

Pap Test vs. HPV Test

A four-part guide

Pap Test vs. HPV Test

Two different tests, often confused.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

The Pap test

Examines cervical cells under a microscope for abnormal changes — signs cells have already started changing.

02

The HPV test

Checks the same sample for high-risk HPV DNA — the underlying cause — rather than looking at the cells.

03

Who, and how often

General guidance: ages 21–29, Pap every 3 years. Ages 30–65, HPV test every 5 years (or Pap every 3, or both together every 5).

Exact intervals vary by country's program — follow the one you're invited for.

04

Why age changes the answer

In the 20s, HPV is common and usually clears on its own — testing for it directly would over-flag harmless infections. After 30, a lingering infection is more meaningful.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

Pap looks at cells. Checks for abnormal changes already present.

HPV looks for the cause. Checks whether high-risk HPV is present.

Age shapes the recommendation. Which test, and how often, depends on age.

Ask your doctor or clinic which test your screening program recommends for your age.

Sources: USPSTF, ACS, WHO (2024–2026). General information, not medical advice.

What Happens During the Exam

A five-part guide

What Happens During the Exam

Fear of the unknown is the biggest barrier. Here's exactly what to expect.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

Arriving & talking with your provider

You'll check in, wait briefly, and be taken to a private room. The provider will ask a few quick health questions and explain the steps — you can ask anything first.

02

The exam room & speculum

You'll undress from the waist down under a sheet or gown, then lie back with your knees supported. A speculum — a smooth instrument — gently holds the vaginal walls open so the cervix is visible.

It can feel like pressure or coolness, but shouldn't be painful.

03

Sample collection, duration & pain

A soft brush wipes cells from the cervix in seconds. The whole visit is usually 10–15 minutes; the sample itself takes under a minute.

Most people feel brief discomfort, not pain. Tell your provider if you're anxious — they can go slower or use a smaller speculum.

04

Afterward

You can resume normal activities immediately. Light spotting is normal. Results typically arrive within a couple of weeks, with next steps explained clearly.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

It's quick. Around 10–15 minutes, most of it just conversation.

Discomfort, not pain. Brief pressure is normal; sharp pain is not — speak up.

Life goes on right after. No recovery time needed for a routine test.

You can always ask for a female provider, a support person, or extra time to feel ready.

General information, not medical advice. Ask your clinic what to expect at your specific appointment.

HPV Positive: What Now?

A four-part guide

HPV Positive: What Now?

A positive result feels alarming. Here's what it actually means.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

HPV positive ≠ cancer

A positive HPV test means the virus was detected — nothing more. It does not mean you have cancer, or that you will get it.

Most HPV-positive results resolve on their own without ever needing treatment.

02 · The pathway

What usually happens next

Depending on the result, a doctor may repeat the test later, or refer you for a closer look. Each step is a chance to catch changes early — not a sign something is already wrong.

03

The likely outcome

Most women with a positive HPV result are monitored, and the infection clears. Only a small number need a colposcopy, and fewer still need any treatment.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

Positive isn't a diagnosis. It means monitoring is needed — not that anything is wrong yet.

There's a clear next step. Repeat testing or colposcopy, decided by your doctor.

Most never develop cancer. The system is designed to catch changes long before that.

If you've tested positive, your next appointment is a precaution, not a verdict.

General information, not medical advice. Always follow your own provider's guidance.

Colposcopy Explained

A four-part guide

Colposcopy Explained

A scary-sounding word for a routine, closer look.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

What it is

A colposcopy uses a magnifying instrument to examine the cervix more closely than a Pap test allows — usually after an abnormal screening result.

02

Does it hurt?

It's similar to a Pap test. A vinegar-like solution highlights abnormal areas and may cause brief stinging or mild cramping.

03

Biopsy

If an area looks abnormal, a tiny tissue sample may be taken for lab testing — usually felt as a brief pinch or cramp, not sharp pain.

04

Duration & recovery

The appointment usually takes 15–20 minutes. Light spotting can follow for a few days; your provider may suggest avoiding tampons or sex briefly if a biopsy was taken.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

It's just a closer look. A more detailed exam, not a treatment.

Discomfort is usually mild. Similar to or slightly more than a Pap test.

Recovery is quick. Most people resume normal activities the same day.

Ask your provider to explain each step as they go — most are glad to.

General information, not medical advice.

Common Myths

Myth vs. fact

Common Myths About Cervical Cancer

Beliefs that quietly keep women from getting screened.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

"No symptoms" & "I'm married"

✕ I have no symptoms, so I'm healthy.

✓ Early cell changes are usually silent — that's exactly why screening exists.

✕ I'm married, so I can't get HPV.

✓ HPV can be carried and passed by a partner from any point in the past — marital status doesn't change risk.

02

"Too old" & "already vaccinated"

✕ I'm too old for this to matter.

✓ Screening guidelines extend to 65 — risk doesn't disappear with age.

✕ I've had the vaccine, so I don't need screening.

✓ The vaccine doesn't cover every risk type — screening is still needed.

03

Embarrassment & access

✕ I'm too embarrassed.

✓ Providers do this every day — you can ask for privacy, a female provider, or to go slowly.

✕ I don't have the right documents, language, or background for this.

✓ Screening is a health service, not a citizenship or language test — interpreters and support are often available.

✕ My husband says I don't need it.

✓ This is your own health decision, and your results are kept confidential.

Key takeaways

The pattern behind every myth

Feeling fine isn't proof. Cervical changes are usually symptom-free until late.

Barriers can be worked around. Privacy, language, and comfort concerns all have solutions.

It's still your decision. No one else's opinion changes what's right for your health.

If one of these thoughts sounds familiar, it's worth asking a provider directly.

General information, not medical advice.

Cervical Cancer Symptoms

A three-part guide

Cervical Cancer Symptoms

What to watch for — and why not to wait for it.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

The symptoms

  • Unusual vaginal bleeding — between periods, after sex, or after menopause
  • Unusual vaginal discharge
  • Pelvic pain
  • Pain during sex

Early precancerous changes usually cause none of these — symptoms tend to appear later.

02

When to seek care

Any of these symptoms — especially bleeding after menopause — deserves a prompt visit to a doctor. Don't wait for them to go away on their own.

03

Why waiting is risky

Symptoms often mean the disease has already advanced past the easily-treatable stage. Screening exists precisely to catch problems before symptoms appear.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

Know the signs. Unusual bleeding, discharge, or pelvic pain are worth checking.

Don't wait it out. See a doctor promptly rather than watching and hoping.

Screening comes first. The goal is catching changes before symptoms ever start.

If something feels off, trust that and get it checked — it's rarely a wasted visit.

General information, not medical advice.

Overcoming Fear

A three-part guide

Overcoming Fear

The emotional barriers are often bigger than the physical ones.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

Embarrassment

It's one of the most common feelings in the room — and providers see it every day. You can ask for privacy, a female provider, or simply more time.

02

Fear of what they'll find

Screening exists to prevent cancer, not diagnose it — most results are normal, and most abnormal results are not cancer either.

03

Fear of pain & general anxiety

Most people feel mild discomfort, not pain. Tell your provider if you're anxious — they can slow down, explain each step, or let you bring someone with you.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

You set the pace. You can ask questions, pause, or request accommodations.

It's about prevention. Most results bring reassurance, not bad news.

Naming the fear helps. Telling your provider you're anxious changes how the visit goes.

The appointment is rarely as difficult as the anticipation of it.

General information, not medical advice.

Pregnancy & Screening

A three-part guide

Pregnancy & Screening

Common questions about timing, answered simply.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

Can I screen while pregnant?

Generally yes — a Pap test is safe during pregnancy and is often included in an early prenatal visit if you're due for one.

02

After giving birth?

If screening is due, providers typically wait until around 12 weeks postpartum before resuming routine testing.

03

While breastfeeding?

Screening is safe during breastfeeding — there's no need to delay or stop nursing for a Pap or HPV test.

Key takeaways

Three things worth remembering

Pregnancy doesn't pause it. Screening can safely happen during pregnancy.

Postpartum has a rhythm. Usually resumes around 12 weeks after birth.

Breastfeeding isn't a barrier. It doesn't need to be stopped or delayed.

If you're due for screening and pregnant or postpartum, ask your provider what timing makes sense for you.

General information, not medical advice.

Menopause & Screening

A two-part guide

Menopause & Screening

A common assumption that isn't quite right.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

The myth

✕ Screening ends automatically at menopause.

✓ Guidelines generally continue screening through age 65, regardless of menopause status.

02

When it can actually stop

Screening can usually stop only after a documented history of adequate, normal results — a decision made with a doctor, not something that happens automatically.

Key takeaways

Two things worth remembering

Menopause isn't a finish line. It doesn't automatically end the need to screen.

Stopping is a decision, not a default. Made with a doctor, based on your screening history.

If you're near or past menopause, ask your doctor whether you're still due.

General information, not medical advice.

Lifestyle & Prevention

A five-part guide

Lifestyle & Prevention

What actually helps — and what it can't replace.

Press play below to begin ▶

01

Smoking

Smoking makes it harder for the immune system to clear HPV, raising risk. Quitting measurably helps.

02

HPV vaccination

The single most effective prevention tool available, when eligible — it prevents the infections that cause most cervical cancers.

03

Safer sex & immune health

Condoms lower HPV transmission risk (without eliminating it), and general immune health supports the body's ability to clear infections naturally.

04

Screening — the constant

None of the above replaces regular screening. It remains the most reliable way to catch problems before they become cancer.

Key takeaways

The honest takeaway

Lifestyle choices help. Not smoking and general health support your immune system.

Vaccination and screening do the heavy lifting. They're the two tools with the strongest evidence.

No lifestyle guarantees safety. Even the healthiest habits don't replace screening.

Combine what you can control with the two things that matter most: vaccination and screening.

General information, not medical advice.

The Screening Journey

One journey, six steps

A Day in the Screening Journey

Following one woman through the whole process, start to finish.

Press play below to begin ▶

01 · The path

From invitation to follow-up

A letter arrives, an appointment gets booked, a short clinic visit happens — and within a few weeks, there's an answer. Most journeys end right there, with normal results.

02

What each step feels like

  • Invitation: a reminder, not an alarm
  • Booking: a five-minute call or a few clicks online
  • Clinic: a short wait, a private room, a quick conversation
  • Test: under a minute of mild discomfort
  • Results: arrive by mail, message, or a follow-up call

Key takeaways

The takeaway

It's a short process. From invitation to result, usually just a few weeks.

Most journeys end simply. A normal result — then the same reminder again in a few years.

Every step is manageable. None of it requires more than an hour of your day.

The hardest part is usually just picking up the phone to book it.

General information, not medical advice.
