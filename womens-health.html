What is Cervical Cancer?

Cervical cancer develops in the cells lining the cervix, usually over many years and almost always linked to persistent HPV infection. Caught early through regular screening, it is one of the most preventable and treatable cancers.

Understanding HPV

HPV is an extremely common virus — most sexually active people are exposed to it at some point, and about 9 in 10 infections clear on their own. Only a small group of high-risk types can lead to cancer if an infection persists for years.

Cervical Cancer Symptoms

Unusual bleeding, discharge or pelvic pain can be warning signs, though early precancerous changes usually cause no symptoms at all — which is why regular screening matters even when you feel completely well.

The HPV Vaccine

The HPV vaccine is one of the most effective tools for preventing the infections that lead to cervical cancer. It's recommended from age 11-12, extensively safety-tested, and works alongside — not instead of — regular screening.

Lifestyle & Prevention

Not smoking, vaccination and general immune health all help lower your risk, but no lifestyle choice replaces regular screening, which remains the most reliable way to catch changes early.

Why Screening Matters

Screening finds and removes precancerous cells before they ever become cancer, checking for early warning signs in people who feel completely healthy — which is what makes it genuinely preventive, not just diagnostic.

Pap Test vs. HPV Test

The Pap test examines cervical cells for abnormal changes, while the HPV test checks for the high-risk virus that causes them. Which test — and how often — depends mainly on your age.

What Happens During the Exam

A step-by-step walkthrough of a routine screening visit, from check-in to the exam itself. It usually takes just 10 to 15 minutes, with at most brief discomfort rather than pain.

The Screening Journey

Follow one woman's path through the entire screening process, from the invitation letter to booking, the test itself, and receiving results, to see just how manageable each step really is.

HPV Positive: What Now?

A positive HPV result means the virus was detected — nothing more. It doesn't mean you have cancer, and most positive results resolve on their own with monitoring alone.

Colposcopy Explained

A colposcopy is simply a closer examination of the cervix after an abnormal screening result — similar to a Pap test, usually with mild discomfort, and over in 15 to 20 minutes.

Pregnancy & Screening

Screening is generally safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding, with testing after birth typically resuming around 12 weeks postpartum.

Menopause & Screening

Screening doesn't automatically stop at menopause — guidelines generally continue through age 65, and stopping earlier is a decision made with a doctor, not a default.

Overcoming Fear

Embarrassment, fear of results and anxiety about discomfort are some of the most common reasons women delay screening — and each one has a practical way through it.

Common Myths

From “no symptoms means I'm healthy” to “I'm too old for this to matter,” this lesson debunks the everyday myths that quietly keep women from getting screened.
