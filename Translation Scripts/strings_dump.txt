158	research	Project Desing and Objective
159	research	Three sub-studies will be conducted
160	research	PROJECT RATIONAL
161	research	Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide. In Italy, an estimated 2,382 new cases were reported in 2024. Nearly 99% of cervical cancer cases are caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV), a very common virus transmitted primarily through sexual contact. In most cases, the infection clears up on its own, but if it persists over time, it can develop into cancer. Fortunately, this is a highly preventable cancer.
162	research	In Italy, the HPV vaccine is free for girls and boys between the ages of 12 and 26. Women between the ages of 25 and 64 are encouraged to participate in screening programs through Pap tests or HPV tests. However, access to these programs is uneven: in the 2021-2022 period, only 77.7% of eligible women participated in screening, with an even lower percentage among immigrant women (72%).
163	research	Immigrants make up more than 8.6% of Italy's resident population. Many immigrant women face language barriers, financial difficulties, and limited knowledge of the healthcare system—factors that hinder their participation in screening and follow-up care, increasing the risk of late diagnoses and, consequently, higher mortality rates.
164	research	STUDY DESIGN
165	research	This project investigates inequalities in cervical cancer prevention among immigrant women in Italy through three complementary substudies: a mixed-methods study, a registry-based study, and a community-based intervention study. The goal is to reduce the incidence of cervical cancer among immigrant women, promote greater participation in screening programs, and provide useful guidance for developing more equitable and inclusive public health strategies.
166	research	The research project seeks to answer three key questions:
167	research	What are the main barriers hindering early diagnosis of cervical cancer in immigrant women?
168	research	Are there differences in the incidence, mortality, and survival rates of cervical cancer between immigrant women and Italian women?
169	research	Can community-based interventions help immigrant women participate more in cervical cancer screening programs?

282	womens-health	What is Cervical Cancer?
283	womens-health	Cervical cancer develops in the cells lining the cervix, usually over many years and almost always linked to persistent HPV infection. Caught early through regular screening, it is one of the most preventable and treatable cancers.
284	womens-health	Understanding HPV
285	womens-health	HPV is an extremely common virus — most sexually active people are exposed to it at some point, and about 9 in 10 infections clear on their own. Only a small group of high-risk types can lead to cancer if an infection persists for years.
286	womens-health	Cervical Cancer Symptoms
287	womens-health	Unusual bleeding, discharge or pelvic pain can be warning signs, though early precancerous changes usually cause no symptoms at all — which is why regular screening matters even when you feel completely well.
288	womens-health	The HPV Vaccine
289	womens-health	The HPV vaccine is one of the most effective tools for preventing the infections that lead to cervical cancer. It's recommended from age 11-12, extensively safety-tested, and works alongside — not instead of — regular screening.
290	womens-health	Lifestyle & Prevention
291	womens-health	Not smoking, vaccination and general immune health all help lower your risk, but no lifestyle choice replaces regular screening, which remains the most reliable way to catch changes early.
292	womens-health	Why Screening Matters
293	womens-health	Screening finds and removes precancerous cells before they ever become cancer, checking for early warning signs in people who feel completely healthy — which is what makes it genuinely preventive, not just diagnostic.
294	womens-health	Pap Test vs. HPV Test
295	womens-health	SThe Pap test examines cervical cells for abnormal changes, while the HPV test checks for the high-risk virus that causes them. Which test — and how often — depends mainly on your age.
296	womens-health	What Happens During the Exam
297	womens-health	A step-by-step walkthrough of a routine screening visit, from check-in to the exam itself. It usually takes just 10 to 15 minutes, with at most brief discomfort rather than pain.
298	womens-health	The Screening Journey
299	womens-health	Follow one woman's path through the entire screening process, from the invitation letter to booking, the test itself, and receiving results, to see just how manageable each step really is.
300	womens-health	HPV Positive: What Now?
301	womens-health	A positive HPV result means the virus was detected — nothing more. It doesn't mean you have cancer, and most positive results resolve on their own with monitoring alone.

305	womens-health	A colposcopy is simply a closer examination of the cervix after an abnormal screening result — similar to a Pap test, usually with mild discomfort, and over in 15 to 20 minutes.
306	womens-health	Pregnancy & Screening
307	womens-health	SScreening is generally safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding, with testing after birth typically resuming around 12 weeks postpartum.
